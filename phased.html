Phased investing: parking proceeds before stocks

Does parking the sale proceeds in short Treasuries for 24 to 36 months soften entry into a crash? Tested on real annual history, 1928 to 2025.

What the test asks

  • Question. If the sale proceeds wait in short Treasuries for 24 or 36 months after the inheritance, and only then move into stocks, how much of a crash that starts at entry is avoided, and what does the wait cost in years with no crash?
  • Method. Real annual history from 1928 to 2025. Cash earns the 3-month Treasury bill return; stocks earn the S&P 500 total return. Each path is followed for 10 years after entry. Tranches are equal and move at the start of each year. flag S-PHASE

Reading: in most entry years the lump sum ends higher, because stocks rose in most years. Phasing reduces the worst falls. That is the trade-off the owner is choosing between.

Reinvestment risk on the parked cash

Parked cash earns whatever bills yield in those years, and that is not fixed. The average 3-month bill return was about 3.4% a year over 1928 to 2025, about 3.3% over 1929 to 1931, and about 0.08% over 2009 to 2015. The parked cash earns no stock growth, so a long wait gives up part of any recovery. How to read it

  • Phasing does not avoid losses that come after the stocks are bought. In the 1929 test the phased paths still fell about 35% from their peak, because they bought stocks in 1931, before the rebound.
  • In the 2007 test the parked cash stayed out of stocks through 2008 and the worst drop was 0%: the wait avoided the crash. Parking was ahead of the lump sum after 10 years.
  • Across the 89 entry years, the phased worst drop was never deeper than the lump sum’s, and it was strictly shallower in 36 of them (24-month park, 2 tranches). The phased path ends higher than the lump sum in only 24% of entry years, so the benefit is protection against one bad entry, not a higher average return.
  • The test uses one entry date per row. A real decision also depends on tax, cash needed for carrying costs, and how much fall in value the owner can accept.

Limits and sources

  • Annual data. A calendar year is one step, so the October 1929 crash appears only in the full-year 1929 return. Intra-year drops are understated.
  • Gross of tax, selling costs, carrying costs and inflation. The Decision page carries those; this page isolates timing.
  • Stock and bill returns come from an academic table, not a government source. This is flagged for review: Flagged for Review.
  • Results are historical. Future sequences can differ; the Black swan page shows named stress paths.
