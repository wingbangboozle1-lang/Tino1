Live, rent or sell

The three paths priced with the same costs, the same tax rules and the same assumptions - then tested against 50 real historical sequences and one-at-a-time sensitivities.

What this page does and does not say. It prices three choices on one set of stated assumptions. It does not know future growth, inflation, tax law, rents or insurance prices. The result is a ranking with a margin, and the margin is what the sensitivity table tests.

Why selling leads: the arithmetic

The basis reset does the work

Inherited property generally takes a basis equal to its value on the date of death, so a sale soon after inheritance produces almost no taxable gain. The model shows a sale at $6,197,490 against a basis of the same value, which is why the gain tax is $0 and the deduction from the sale is mostly the 5.0% selling cost.

The alternative is a concentrated single asset

Renting keeps one house as the whole of the marginal position: one structure, one housing market, one insurance market, and rent that is capped in law. A diversified fund spreads the same money across hundreds of businesses. The model rewards that diversification at 7.0% a year, which is an academic historical average, not a government statistic and not a promise.

The costs the sale avoids

These are running costs in the transfer year, not the difference against living in the home: the owner pays the same property tax and insurance whichever of the three paths is chosen. What selling avoids is the operating cost of holding a home that produces nothing.

Read the rent break-even carefully. Renting would need a monthly rent of $16,650 to match selling on the baseline. The HUD benchmark for a 3-bedroom in the same ZIP code is $5,870, and HUD itself describes that figure as a 40th-percentile fair-market benchmark, not a valuation of any particular house. The gap between the two is the size of the case for selling.

Risk: the same comparison replayed 50 times

The single baseline is one invented future. A better test is to replay the real 1976-2025 record - actual inflation, actual house-price growth, actual stock and bill returns - starting from each of its 50 years in turn. That keeps the order of crises rather than averaging them away.

The honest caveat. A replay of 1976-2025 is not a probability. It is 50 observations drawn from one history, and the future may contain a sequence that history does not. What it does show is that the result does not depend on one lucky decade: selling wins every twenty-year window in the sample, and loses some ten-year ones. Any plan that needs the money within ten years is taking real risk.

The recommendation, stated as a plan

  1. Before anything else: read the trust

    Confirm whether the home will be in the transferor's gross estate and whether the trust or the inheritor will be the owner. If the home is outside the gross estate the basis does not reset and the ranking can flip. This is Q01 and Q03.

  2. Do not sell Property B

    It is the housing baseline and the income baseline. Its assessed value is low precisely because it has not changed hands, and that advantage is permanent if it is kept. Keep living in the third unit; the model assumes the same occupancy in every path.

  3. At the transfer: file the claims inside the statutory windows

    The homeowners' exemption and the Proposition 19 exclusion have different deadlines and both are needed for the relief to hold. The process page lists them in order.

  4. Sell within the year, and sell once

    The basis reset is at its most valuable immediately: every month of ownership after the transfer adds exposure to the market without adding to the basis. $6,152,510 is released on the baseline. Selling costs are already deducted.

  5. Invest the proceeds, in stages

    The comparison is against a diversified stock fund at the historical average, not against cash. Holding a few years of spending in short-term Treasury bills while investing the rest in stages narrows the ten-year risk the replays expose.

  6. Re-check the four decisive facts every year

    Estate size, portability, the Proposition 19 allowance and the trust's terms all change the answer. They are listed on the questions page with a discovery route for each.
