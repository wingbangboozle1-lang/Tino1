What the table shows

The tax bill, not the house price, is the moving part

Property B's assessed value grows at the 2% cap while its market value grows faster, so the gap between the two widens every year. By 2076 the assessed value is $313,680 against a market value of $12,792,030. That gap is the reason Property B is the anchor of the plan: it is a permanent, compounding tax advantage that exists only for as long as the property is not sold.

Property A's bill jumps at the transfer

Before the transfer A is taxed on $802,030. In the transfer year the rent-out assessment is $6,531,230 and the tax is $77,480. Because the roll keeps growing at the 2% cap, the tax reaches about $112,220 a year by 2060 and about $166,590 by 2076. Living in it holds the assessment to $5,031,080 and the first-year tax to $59,950, because the Proposition 19 relief applies - but only if the claims are filed in time and the home really is the inheritor's principal residence.

The single biggest weakness in this table is insurance. No property-specific quote has been obtained for either home. The figures shown are the owner's estimates rounded to $10. A California homeowners policy excludes earthquake, flood, mold and losses after a long vacancy unless separate cover is bought, and a landlord policy is a different product from a homeowners policy. Treat every premium figure here as a placeholder and replace it with a quote before deciding.
