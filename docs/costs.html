The solar changes the usual arithmetic. A Bay Area single-family home of this size would normally spend $2,000–$3,600 a year on electricity. Because the array over-produces, this property runs a small annual credit instead. That is worth roughly $180 a year directly, but more importantly it removes the fastest-growing line item on a California utility bill. Note that the credit is a net-metering construct and its value is set by the applicable tariff, which the CPUC has been modifying — treat the credit as likely to shrink, not grow.

Property B is the engine of this plan. Even after inheriting and being fully reassessed, it produces positive cash flow in every projected year. That cash flow is what makes holding it easy and what funds the carrying costs of Property A. The rent is rent-controlled, so it grows slowly, but it is durable.

Insurance: the weakest number in this analysis

Both insurance figures are owner estimates, entered as $1,500 per year. They are used because there is no better source available to this analysis, and they are flagged as unverified. The NAIC's published national average premium for the most common homeowners form was $1,655 in California for 2023 — which makes $1,500 plausible for Property A.

Property B is very likely under-insured in this model. A $1,500 premium is plausible for a single-family home. It is not plausible for a three-unit San Francisco building with replacement cost well above $1 million, insured on a landlord policy that must also cover loss of rents and higher liability limits. A realistic figure is several times higher. Because this understates Property B's costs, it slightly overstates the case for keeping it — but the error is on the order of a few thousand dollars a year against a decision worth millions, so it does not change the conclusion. Earthquake is not in these numbers

Neither estimate appears to include earthquake coverage, which is a separate policy from the California Earthquake Authority or a private insurer and is materially expensive. Both properties sit in a region of significant seismic hazard. If earthquake coverage is held, these carrying costs are too low by a wide margin. If it is not held, that is a large uninsured exposure that belongs in the decision, not outside it.
