Property Tax

California property tax is governed by two constitutional amendments that pull in opposite directions at inheritance: Proposition 13 keeps tax low by capping the assessed value, and Proposition 19 decides whether that low basis survives a transfer to the next generation. For these two properties, Proposition 19 is worth roughly $600,210 over 25 years — but only if an heir actually moves in.

1. The baseline: Proposition 13

Proposition 13 is codified in article XIII A of the California Constitution and implemented by Revenue & Taxation Code section 51. It does two things:

  • Caps the tax rate at 1% of taxable value, plus voter-approved bonded debt. The 1% figure is the general levy; the actual composite rate on a bill is higher.
  • Caps annual growth of the assessed value at the rate of inflation, and never more than 2% per year, regardless of what the market does.

Critically, the assessed value resets to market value on a change in ownership or completed new construction. Between those events it is frozen at the acquisition value plus at most 2% per year. That gap — between a decades-old assessed value and today's market value — is the entire value of Proposition 13 to a long-held owner.

2. The pivotal rule: Proposition 19

Proposition 19 (2020) replaced the old Propositions 58/193 parent-child exclusion on 16 February 2021. It narrowed the exclusion dramatically: a low Prop 13 basis now survives a parent-to-child transfer only for a family home that becomes the child's own principal residence.

This is the single most important fact in the analysis. Before 2021, a child could inherit a rental property and keep the parent's assessed value forever. Since 16 February 2021 that is gone. The Santa Clara County Assessor states it directly: an intergenerational transfer of a rental home does not qualify for the exclusion. SCC Assessor SCC

The four conditions, all of which must be met

  1. The property was the transferor's principal residence. It must have been eligible for the homeowners' or disabled veterans' exemption in the parent's name. BOE LTA 2026/026 BOE
  2. It becomes the transferee's principal residence within one year. There are "no exceptions to this rule." BOE LTA 2026/026 BOE
  3. The heir files for the homeowners' or disabled veterans' exemption within one year of the transfer. Missing this forfeits the exclusion entirely. BOE-19-P / BOE Prop 19
  4. Market value at transfer does not exceed the transferor's factored base year value plus $1,044,586 (the 2025–27 figure). Any excess is added to the new taxable value. BOE LTA 2025/009 BOE

The $1 million cap is not $1 million

The statute indexes the amount every two years against the FHFA House Price Index for California. This is frequently missed:

The exclusion is conditional and reversible. The Board of Equalization is explicit: the family home must be continually used as the heir's principal residence. "Once the property is no longer your principal residence, it will receive a new taxable value as of the lien date following the date you no longer occupy the property." If the heir moves out — even years later — the low basis is revoked retroactively to the date of the move, and the resulting tax is computed on the value at the original transfer date, indexed forward. BOE LTA 2026/026 BOE That makes "move in for a while, then rent it" a trap: it earns the exclusion for the occupancy period, then loses it with a large catch-up.

4. Property B — San Francisco, in detail

Property B is a different animal: three livable units, two currently rented. This has three consequences that matter.

Consequence one: it will be fully reassessed on inheritance

Because it is a rental (and because Proposition 19 covers only one principal residence per heir, which will be spent on Property A if the family goes that route), Property B gets no exclusion. Its assessed value resets to market. The tax change is dramatic:

Consequence three: rent control pins the growth

San Francisco's Rent Ordinance caps annual increases for covered units at 60% of the Bay Area CPI increase. The current allowable increase is 1.6%, effective 1 March 2026 through 28 February 2027. SF Rent Board SF Units not covered by the local ordinance still fall under the statewide AB 1482 cap of 5% + CPI, hard-capped at 10%. Civ. Code §1947.12 CA Law

The practical effect: Property B's rent will very likely grow slower than inflation. Modeled at 1.6% against 3.0% general inflation, its real rent declines about 1.4% per year. That is a strong argument for keeping it for its locked-in basis and low tax, and a strong argument against treating it as a growth asset.

Also note: under Proposition 19 an heir may claim the intergenerational exclusion on only one principal residence. If the family occupies Property A to preserve its basis, Property B is automatically reassessed. The two exclusions compete. BOE LTA 2026/026 BOE
