Scenarios & Sensitivity

Three options, twelve assumption sets, one answer. This page shows the full sensitivity grid so the conclusion can be checked rather than taken on trust — and so the specific conditions under which it would flip are visible.

Method

All three scenarios are evaluated on the same basis so the comparison is fair:

  1. Same starting conditions. Inheritance in 2045. Both properties held or disposed of according to the scenario. Property B is retained and continues to be rented in all three.
  2. Same horizon. 2070. Everything is liquidated at the end, so no scenario is favoured by holding an appreciating asset "forever."
  3. Real tax on exit. Sale costs of 5.0%, plus capital gains on the difference between sale price and stepped-up basis. Federal long-term rate plus the 3.8% net investment income tax; California at a 9.3% marginal rate. The Section 121 home-sale exclusion of $500,000 is applied only where the property was actually occupied as a principal residence.
  4. Idle cash is invested, not spent. Operating cash flow in every scenario is swept into a portfolio returning 6.5% net of a 0.5% tax drag.
  5. Two measures. Net worth at 2070 is the terminal number. NPV at 2045 discounts everything back at the portfolio rate, which is the theoretically correct way to compare streams of different shapes.

Why both measures. Net worth at 2070 rewards anything that compounds for a long time and can be dominated by terminal value. NPV is the right ranking tool because it accounts for the time value of everything in between. The two agree in every case here, which is a useful check on robustness.

The three options defined

Live in Property A

An heir moves in within one year and claims the homeowners' exemption, preserving the parent's Prop 13 base under Proposition 19. Property B's third unit is freed up and rented, so all three SF units produce income.

Cash flow: all SF rent in, no Cupertino rent.
Tax: Property A at $97,570 in 2070.

Rent out Property A

The family stays in San Francisco. Property A becomes a rental, which means Proposition 19 does not apply and it is reassessed to full market value. Cupertino rent comes in; San Francisco rent stays at two units.

Cash flow: Cupertino rent in, SF at two units.
Tax: Property A at $126,790 in 2070 — $29,220 more.

Sell Property A

Sold shortly after inheritance. Basis equals date-of-death value, so there is no capital gain and no tax. Proceeds go into a diversified portfolio; the family lives in San Francisco.

Cash flow: SF rent at two units plus portfolio income.
Proceeds: $6,204,670 after costs.

Base case

Selling produces $21.71M more wealth by 2070

That is 111% more than living in the house. The gap is not driven by appreciation assumptions — it survives every combination tested below. It is arithmetic. In the transfer year the house costs $87,410 a year to hold against a $6.53M value, which is a negative 1.34% operating yield. Add the 4.0% appreciation and the gross return is 2.66%. The portfolio returns 6.5% net. The house has to appreciate far faster than it has been to close that spread.

What the sale case depends on

The sale recommendation rests on three conditions, and each should be tested before acting:

  1. The proceeds are actually reinvested. If the money is spent or held in cash, the entire advantage disappears. This is a behavioural commitment, not a market fact.
  2. A 6.5% net portfolio return is achieved. At a 5% return the break-even appreciation rate falls to 7.2%, which is closer to the recent five-year trend. Still above it, but the margin narrows substantially.
  3. No rent-yield surprise. If Cupertino single-family rent were materially higher relative to price than modeled, the rental case improves. HUD's FY2026 fair market rent for a 2-bedroom in Santa Clara County ($3,483/mo) is the basis used, giving gross yield well under 2% on a $6.53M house.
