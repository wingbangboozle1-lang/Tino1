Flagged for Review

This page lists everything that could not be confirmed against an official source. These items do not block the analysis and they do not change the recommendation — but each one is a place where a real document would replace an estimate.

Why this page exists. The instruction was explicit: if a value cannot be confirmed from an official source, leave it blank and flag it, and never infer or fill in values. Several figures needed for this analysis are known only to the family — market values, rent roll, utility costs. Those are shown as sourced to "Owner" and flagged here rather than being silently presented as fact. Where an official statue, regulator publication, or agency dataset did confirm a figure, it is marked Verified and not listed here.

2. Irregularities in the supplied figures

These are cases where the numbers provided internally do not reconcile with the official rates this analysis verified. They are presented here rather than adjusted away.

Irregularity 1 — Property A implied effective tax rate is high

MeasureValueBasis
Tax paid 2025$7,207Owner record

Typical SCC ad valorem rate1.1488%SCC Assessor Booklet SCC
Tax at that rate$6,201539,743 × 1.1488%
Difference$1,006Unexplained by the typical rate

Assessment. An effective rate of 1.335% is high but not impossible — tax rate areas vary, and the parcel may sit in one with more school or special district bonded debt, or carry fixed direct assessments such as a school parcel tax or a lighting district. The model therefore applies the verified 1.1488% rate to assessed value and carries the residual $1,006 as a fixed charge that inflates. Rebuilt this way the model produces $7,330 for 2025 against an actual $7,207 — a 1.7% variance, which is a reasonable reconciliation.

What would resolve it: the actual 2025–26 tax bill, which itemises the general levy, each bond, and each fixed assessment separately. This is a one-line lookup on the county tax collector's site.

Irregularity 2 — Property B implied effective tax rate is very high

MeasureValueBasis

Assessment. This one is harder to explain. An implied rate of 2.135% is roughly 1.8× the San Francisco secured rate. Some of the gap will be fixed direct assessments and special district charges, which bulk larger as a proportion when the assessed value is very low — a $1,089 fixed charge against a $114,248 base looks enormous but is normal in absolute terms. That is the most likely explanation. It cannot be confirmed without the bill.

Handling in the model: the $1,089 is held as a fixed dollar amount that grows with inflation, and the official 1.1816% rate is applied to assessed value. This is conservative for the decision being evaluated, because it slightly understates Property B's post-reassessment tax. Property B's tax is projected to jump from $2,490 to $46,720 at inheritance. If the true fixed charges are higher, that number is higher.

What would resolve it: the 2025–26 San Francisco secured tax bill.

Irregularity 3 — assessed values appear to be split across roll years

The Property A table runs to a 2026 figure of $550,537 and the Property B table to a 2025 figure of $114,248. The analysis rolls Property B forward to 2026–27 at 2% to place both on the same footing. This is disclosed rather than silent, because comparing a 2026 roll to a 2025 roll without adjusting would introduce a small error.

Irregularity 4 — the two properties' property-tax series use different year ranges

The Property A series begins in 2014 and the Property B series in 2016. This has no effect on the analysis, which needs only the current assessed value and the growth mechanism, not the history. Noted for completeness.

4. Nothing on this page blocks the recommendation

The largest unverified item is the market value of Property A — but the conclusion does not depend on it. The break-even analysis shows that holding the house beats selling only if it appreciates faster than 9.5% per year for 25 years. That threshold is a rate, not a dollar amount, so it is unaffected by whether the house is worth $2.8 million or $3.4 million. Getting a real appraisal would sharpen every number on the site; it would not change which column comes out ahead.

What to do with this page. Each of the nine unverified items has a specific document listed against it. Collecting all of them would take a family perhaps two hours and would let every projection on this site be rebuilt with verified inputs. That is the right next step before acting on any of it.
