Executive summary

The complete analysis in one page. Figures are nominal dollars unless marked, rounded to the nearest $10, from a transparent model whose inputs are listed on the Methodology page.

1. Situation

The Heir expects to inherit, through the Transferor's revocable trust, sometime between 2040 and 2050:

  • Property A — single-family home in Cupertino (ZIP 95014), estimated value $3,100,000 (Nov 2026, user estimate). Low Prop 13 assessed value (about $539,740 in 2025, user-supplied) and a 2025 tax bill of about $7,210. Solar (net electricity credit), water $100–150/month, lower maintenance.
  • Property B — three-unit building in San Francisco's Outer Sunset (ZIP 94122), estimated value $1,800,000. Two units rented for a combined $7,150/month; one unit vacant and available for the Heir. Water $330–400/month, electricity $30–70/month.

The model assumes Property A is the Transferor's principal residence and that both properties are held 100% in the trust (both assumptions are flagged because they change the answer).

3. What happens at inheritance (base case: death in 2045)

  • Estate tax: the two properties are projected at $6,531,230 (A) and $3,156,310 (B) — a combined $9,687,540, well under a projected per-person exclusion of about $23,979,750. Only if the Transferor's other assets exceed roughly $14,292,210 (single) would federal estate tax (40% on the excess) arise. No California estate tax. See Estate tax.
  • Income tax: both properties receive a stepped-up basis equal to date-of-death value; selling within a year or so produces essentially no capital-gains tax.
  • Property tax, Property A: if the Heir moves in within one year, the Prop 19 exclusion limits the new assessed value to $4,979,030 instead of $6,531,230 — a saving of about $18,060 per year — but the bill still rises from $10,500 (2044) to $59,240 (2045). If the Heir does not move in, the bill is $77,380.
  • Property tax, Property B: all three units are reassessed to market value; the bill becomes about $38,750 per year (from a very low Prop 13 base today).
  • Rents: existing tenancies in B continue under rent control (if the building pre-dates 1979 — unverified); the vacant unit can be rented at market (about $3,500/month today, HUD benchmark) or occupied by the Heir.

5. Recommendation and reasoning

Best plan to maximize after-tax wealth and minimize cost (base case): S4 — Live in B's vacant unit, sell A and invest. Close second: S6 — Sell both at inheritance, invest, rent elsewhere ($14,023,630).

  1. Sell Property A soon after inheritance. The stepped-up basis makes the sale almost tax-free (only post-death appreciation is taxed), and it eliminates a carrying cost of about $75,780 per year in 2045 (property tax $59,240 even with Prop 19, plus insurance, water and maintenance). Net proceeds of about $6,197,490 invested at 7% earn about $433,824 a year — far more than the house's expected appreciation of 4% minus its costs. Prop 19's capped exclusion only saves about $18,060/yr, not enough to change this.
  2. Keep Property B and live in the vacant unit if the Heir is content with that home: it provides rent-free housing, two rent-controlled units produce modest net income (about $31,100 after costs in 2045 before income tax), and B's land in San Francisco keeps appreciating. If the Heir does not want to live in B, selling both and renting (S6, $14,023,630) is nearly as good as S4 and avoids landlord obligations under San Francisco's rent ordinance.
  3. If living in Property A is a priority, choose S2 (live in A, sell B and invest the proceeds: $12,218,600). It costs about $2,000,160 of 2055 wealth versus the best plan — the price of living in a $6,531,230 house whose market rent is about $134,460/yr. Move in and file the Homeowners' Exemption within one year of death to keep the Prop 19 exclusion; staying at least two years also unlocks the $250,000/$500,000 home-sale exclusion on later gains.
  4. Do not hold both properties as pure rentals (S5): it is the worst outcome in every sensitivity case — after reassessment, net rental yields are about 2–3% of value while the Heir also pays rent elsewhere.
  5. Do not accept a lifetime gift of either property. A gift carries over the Transferor's low basis (no step-up), triggers the same Prop 19 reassessment rules, and uses estate/gift exclusion. Inheritance through the trust is strictly better.

When the answer flips: keeping Property A (S1) wins only when home prices rise ≥6%/yr and portfolio returns are ≤5%/yr. The ranking is insensitive to the inheritance year (2040/2045/2050).

6. Actions now (2026–2039), by the Transferor and Heir together

  1. Confirm the trust is a revocable living trust holding title to both properties (avoids probate; preserves the §1014 step-up). Keep the Homeowners' Exemption on Property A in the Transferor's name (it evidences the "family home").
  2. Keep records: purchase documents, improvements and depreciation on B (needed for the final returns and the trust's Form 1041 if rents are earned before distribution).
  3. Decide now whether the Heir would realistically move into A within 12 months of death — the exclusion has no grace period. If not, plan for a sale and for roughly $77,380/yr of tax while the house is marketed (2045 base case).
  4. Verify the flagged inputs that matter most: B's build year / rent-control status, both insurance premiums, and which property carries the Homeowners' Exemption. See Flagged for Review.
  5. Re-check the law every two years: Prop 19 cap adjustment (next Feb 16 2027), the federal exclusion (any Congress can change it), certified results of Props 3 and 42, and the Empty Homes Tax litigation. See Pending changes.
