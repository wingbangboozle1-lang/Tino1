Why this section exists. The main projections assume smooth growth (≈2.5% inflation, 3–4% appreciation, every year). No 50-year period of US economic history ever looked like that. This section re-runs the whole decision — live in the Cupertino house, rent it out, or sell it and reinvest — under economic paths built from what actually happened: stagflation, a Bay Area home-price crash of −23% to −35%, deflation, and a pandemic shock. Every historical figure below links to its official source (BLS, NBER, FHFA, Freddie Mac, CBO, BOE); every modeling assumption is marked assumption or derived. All dollar amounts are rounded to $10.

Reading the grid. SELL wins 12 of 18 cells — the same answer as the smooth baseline, but for a more robust reason: selling at the step-up converts a single undiversified asset into a diversified portfolio at exactly the moment the basis resets to zero gain. LIVE wins the remaining 3 cells only where a housing boom lands right after inheritance while stocks are replaying weak years (REPLAY 2045/2050). RENT never wins: full reassessment at death plus ordinary-rate rent tax erase the legacy Prop 13 advantage in every path tested.

Deep-think: what each black swan does to the decision

Stagflation (1974–83 pattern). The house is the best inflation hedge in the portfolio: its nominal price tracks inflation while the Prop 13 tax bill (≤2%/yr growth — BOE) is paid in devalued dollars. But selling still edges ahead in the grid, because the assumed stock path is merely flat, not destroyed. If you believe a stagflation decade is likely, the LIVE option tightens the gap: you consume housing services you would otherwise rent, at a fixed tax cost.
GFC-style Bay Area crash (−35%). Timing beats magnitude. Crash before death → the step-up basis and the estate value both reset lower; selling at inheritance still wins (recovery accrues inside the diversified portfolio, tax-free to date). Crash after inheritance → holding concentrates the loss in one asset while the sell-side money is already diversified. Do not sell into the crash year voluntarily; the step-up at death removes the old gain, so selling early is never tax-urgent.
Deflation (Japan-style tail). Everything loses; SELL merely loses least (cash and bonds do not deflate; the −2%/yr property spiral is the worst asset to hold). Property tax cannot fall below the factored base year value — the Prop 8 floor sits near $550K+, far under any modeled value, so the bust's assessment relief is exactly zero for this property.
COVID-shock whiplash (+18% then 8% inflation then rate shock). A demand boom at the wrong moment flatters the keep-side numbers, but the grid still favors SELL at 2040/2045/2050 because the reinvested portfolio captures the rebound. Sequence-of-returns risk on the sell side is real: if the stock crash lands in the first years after selling, terminal wealth falls even when the long-run average is fine — see the sensitivity grid on the Scenarios page.

Estate exposure varies wildly with the shock window (death 2045)

The same two houses are worth far less — or far more — at death depending only on which economic decade precedes it (see the values column above, $5.6M to $15.5M across scenarios at death 2045). A crash year is a natural estate-tax cushion; a boom year is not. The owner's additional cash assets (not modeled here — see Estate tax for the 0–$30M grid) decide whether the 40% federal rate applies. Recommended decision rule (black-swan aware)

  1. Default: sell the Cupertino house shortly after inheritance and reinvest (stepped-up basis → ~zero capital-gains tax; 1%–5% selling/transfer costs). This wins in 12/18 stress cells and in the smooth baseline. Confirm the 2-of-5-year §121 timing with a CPA if you instead move in first.
  2. Switch to LIVE-in (move in ≤1 year) only if (a) you want Bay Area housing services anyway and (b) a housing boom with weak stocks is the credible path — the Prop 19 partial shield (cap ≈ $1.04M indexed — BOE Prop 19) plus B's vacant unit rent roughly doubles the keep-side case in the REPLAY boom cells.
  3. Never RENT the Cupertino house out under any scenario tested: full reassessment at market (~$77K/yr at 2045 values) plus ordinary-rate rent tax outweigh the income. (Cupertino rent-control status assumed absent — assumption.)
  4. If a crash is in progress at the death date: do not panic-sell; sell anyway when orderly — the step-up already eliminated the gain, and the grid's GFC rows show selling at inheritance still beats holding through the recovery.
  5. Keep the SF triplex untouched throughout — its low assessment (2016-era base) and existing rent streams are identical across strategies and were the single best inflation hedge in every path.
