SEQUENCE RISK / 05

Staged entry after a crash

If the sale proceeds arrive at the wrong moment, does parking them in short Treasuries and phasing into stocks soften the damage? Tested on real annual history, with the cash leg taken from official Federal Reserve series.

What this page is, and what it is not

This is the only page in the guide built from real annual history rather than an invented stress shape. The stock leg is an academic series, not a government one; the cash leg is official Federal Reserve data. It tests one question only: whether waiting and phasing in softens entry risk when the proceeds arrive at the start of a bad year. It contains no household amount, and it is not a forecast, a probability or a recommendation.

Read this before the tables. Each trigger can reverse the ranking of a lump sum and a staged entry, and each links to the rule that governs it.

The episode entries the brief asks about

Each run starts on 1 January of the entry year with the same amount and finishes ten years later. Lump sum buys stocks immediately. Staged holds everything in the cash leg for three years, then moves it into stocks in three equal annual tranches. The parked leg earns the official annual average; where an official series does not reach back far enough, the gap is closed with an explicit 0% assumption shown in the row, never with an invented yield. The dollar columns use a $100,000 illustrative amount; the percentages are the part that travels.

Read the 1929 row carefully. The staged path ends far ahead, and the effect is not subtle: the lump sum sat through −25.1%, −43.8% and −8.6% before the recovery and ends at $84,500 against $206,290. But that run parks at an assumed 0%, so the gap is a floor for the staged advantage, not a measured result, and it is a large one: about 2.4 times the lump sum. 2008 is the opposite case: one crash year is followed at once by +25.9%, so cash still parked misses most of the rebound and the staged advantage is small (+5.4% over the lump sum). 1973 is the case that best shows why a wait can be paid for: stocks fell two years running while official 3-month bills averaged 6.88% a year.

Reinvestment risk sits beside the headline

Two risks sit on either side of these numbers. (1) The wait itself: parking for 24-36 months means the money is reinvested later at unknown rates, and a rising market during the wait is a permanent cost, not a timing difference. Across the 82 entry years where every value is official, staging lost in 68 and won in 14. (2) The parked cash: a bill rolled every three months has an unknown rate at each roll, and the official annual average bill rate ranged from 0.03% (2014) to 14.03% (1981). If the parked money earns less than inflation, the wait is a real loss before a single stock is bought. Neither is a probability, and this page assigns no odds.

Three findings hold across every rule tested. Staging is usually behind: in the median entry year the staged path ends 29% below the lump sum, because cash for 24-36 months normally earns less than stocks. The extremes are wide: the worst entry year (1995) ends 58% below, and the best (2000) 48% above. A longer horizon does not rescue it (20-year median −27.7%; worst 1995: −57.5%), because more time gives a rising market more time to punish the parked years. And in the 20 entry years that fell, staging won only 7 times: a crash year is a necessary condition for staging to help, not a sufficient one, because the rebound can arrive before the tranches are spent.

The first table reports principal at a $100,000 starting amount; this one reports the same gap as a percentage of the lump-sum endpoint. They rank the years the same way but the sizes differ, so do not add the two columns together.

Limits, stated plainly

Calendar years only, so a crash inside a year (October 1929, the autumn of 2008) appears only through that year's total return. Arithmetic annual returns, not daily compounding. No tax, fees, fund expenses or inflation, so the tax and inflation rows above say which way that biases the result. The stock leg is a non-government academic series and the cash legs are official; the 2-year leg is an annual average yield, not a holding return with price changes, so no ladder total return is claimed. Years before 1934 use an explicit 0% parking assumption. Nothing here says a 24-36 month wait is optimal, and no probability is attached to any path.

What would make this page wrong for the household

A different tax situation, a need for the money inside the wait, a plan to spend the proceeds on housing instead of stocks, or a cash holding that is not a Treasury (a brokered deposit, a money market fund, an insured account) would each change the arithmetic. Those facts are private and are not published here.
