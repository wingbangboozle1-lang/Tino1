Transparent stress paths

Begin with unrelated investment capital of $1,000,000. Smooth reference: 5% effective net return, 2.5% inflation for 50 years. Crash: −40% in year 1, then 5%. Inflation shock: 3% return and 8% inflation for five years, then 5% / 2.5%. Stagnation: 0% for ten years, then 5%, with 2.5% inflation throughout. Late crash: 5% except −40% in year 20; inflation 2.5%. No contributions, withdrawals or separate taxes; returns are assumed net of all tax/fees. These are invented stresses, not historical replays, predictions or probabilities.

Official economic readings · current snapshot, not a 50-year baseline

Observed conditions vary by period. The table gives dated indicators, not forecasts or local-property estimates. The latest available 12-month CPI readings are for September: the October 2026 release reports month-to-month changes but says most 12-month comparisons cannot be calculated because October 2025 data were unavailable. A long-horizon model should therefore test regimes and update the inputs, not compound one recent figure as if it were stable.

Sequence-risk distinction

With no cash flows, the same returns in a different order have the same terminal product. Early and late crash paths therefore match at year 50. With tax payments, repairs or withdrawals, sequence matters: selling depressed assets can leave fewer assets to participate in recovery. Do not claim this lump-sum illustration measures withdrawal sequence risk.

Reinvestment risk beside the headline

The parking return is a flat assumed 3% per year, not a Treasury quote or yield forecast; the what-if table varies it from 2% to 4%. Actual 3-month-to-2-year yields and maturity-date reinvestment rates can differ. Staging benefits these chosen early-crash paths but lags immediate equity entry in the rising-market path; it does not remove market loss, assure a higher return, or make a 24–36 month wait optimal. The official links give historical / diversification context only and do not validate the synthetic returns. For the same question tested on the real annual record, with the cash leg taken from official Federal Reserve series, see Staged entry after a crash; that page also shows that in the median historical entry year staging ended behind a lump sum.

A single number hides the range: outcome percentiles

Each table above shows one path. A reader who sees only a compounding result will underestimate how far an outcome can land from it. The table below keeps the same invented discipline and adds dispersion: $1,000,000 of unrelated capital, 20,001 simulated paths, and in every path an independent annual return drawn from a normal distribution with an invented 5% mean and 17% standard deviation. Inflation is a fixed invented 2.5%. No tax, fees or withdrawals. The seed is fixed, so two builds produce the same numbers. This is arithmetic on stated assumptions: it is not a calibrated forecast, it assigns no probability to any real outcome, and it is not advice.

How to read the band

The single compound-at-5% figure that most projections show sits above the median of this invented spread, and well above the 10th percentile. The 10th percentile at 30 years is below the $1,000,000 starting amount in nominal terms, before tax and before adjusting for inflation. None of these numbers is evidence about real odds: the mean and the spread are invented rather than measured. The practical rule is that a decision which only works at the 75th or 90th percentile does not work at the median, so the band belongs beside every single-point figure.
