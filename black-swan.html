Black swan section: non-constant growth and inflation over 50 years

What happens to the decision when inflation, home prices and markets behave the way they really have: in bursts, busts and long flat stretches.

This section is kept apart from the baseline on purpose. The baseline uses constant rates so that the three choices can be compared cleanly. Here nothing is constant: every path uses real recorded years, in their real order, for inflation, local home prices, stocks, bonds and bills.

Each replay keeps the real year-to-year order, so inflation, home prices and stocks move together as they actually did. The spread is very wide because the order of good and bad years matters: a boom just after the sale helps the seller, a crash just after hurts. Ten years after the inheritance selling is behind renting in 7 of the 50 histories, by as much as $2,069,690; after twenty years it is ahead in every one. The 1976-2025 period was an unusually good one for United States stocks, which is why the Depression and Japan paths below are shown as well.

The Depression path uses national home-price data because no local index goes back that far (flag S02). Selling into stocks ends behind renting in two of the named paths: the 1929-1941 Depression sequence (renting $4,960,970 against $4,757,310 for selling) and Japan-style stagnation (renting $4,293,520 against $3,938,260), the worse of the two. In the Japan path, had the proceeds been kept in savings instead of stocks, selling would have ended at $3,799,310 against $3,550,260 for renting (flag S08).
